# Wireless CarPlay Adapter Distributor Agreement Checklist

**Direct answer:** A wireless CarPlay adapter distributor agreement should connect commercial rights to one controlled product configuration. Before signing, both parties should define the territory and channels, approved SKUs and claims, forecast and order rules, pricing and payment assumptions, packaging and regulatory responsibilities, delivery handover, warranty and technical escalation, data handling, and the change-notification process. The agreement is not a promise that every vehicle will work; it is the operating framework for selling, supporting and replenishing an evidence-defined product.

This checklist is for automotive-electronics distributors, importers, private-label brands and OEM/ODM buyers. It is an operational guide, not legal advice. Local competition, consumer, tax, product-safety, privacy and contract requirements differ by market, so qualified local counsel should review the final agreement.

## Why a distributor agreement needs product controls

A distributor agreement is often treated as a territory and price document. That is too narrow for a connected-car accessory. A wireless adapter may look identical across two offers while the firmware branch, cable, pack-out, manual, compatibility wording or support route differs. If those differences are not attached to the commercial relationship, sales teams can publish claims that the supplier cannot trace back to the shipped product.

Start by defining the product family in the [wireless CarPlay adapter product center](/products/?category=CarPlay%20Adapters#catalog). A standard adapter normally converts an existing compatible factory wired-CarPlay path to wireless use; it does not add CarPlay to every USB-equipped vehicle. That prerequisite belongs in the product record, listing copy, reseller briefing and support workflow—not only in a technical appendix.

The agreement should point to controlled schedules rather than attempting to freeze every technical detail in its main body. A SKU schedule can identify the commercial model, hardware revision, approved firmware baseline, connector or cable, included accessories, packaging version, languages and target markets. When either party changes an item, the schedule creates a clear question: does the change require approval, new content, revalidation, separate inventory identity or all four?

## 1. Define the parties, territory and channel scope

Write down who may sell, where and through which routes. Territory is not merely a country name: a distributor may sell through its own warehouse, authorized resellers, installers, marketplaces, fleet partners or cross-border e-commerce. Each route changes the visibility of compatibility statements, return obligations and customer data.

Clarify whether the appointment is non-exclusive, exclusive, conditional, or limited to particular channels. If exclusivity is discussed, avoid treating it as an automatic reward for a forecast. Define the evidence needed to maintain it, the review period, exceptions for existing accounts, handling of unsolicited inbound business, and the remedy if either party misses a condition. These are commercial decisions for the parties and counsel, not a universal template.

| Scope item | Questions to settle | Evidence to attach |
|---|---|---|
| Territory | Which countries, regions or customer accounts are included? | Written territory schedule and map where helpful |
| Channels | May the distributor sell to resellers, installers or marketplaces? | Approved channel list and marketplace policy |
| Brand use | Which product names, images and marks may be used? | Brand and asset-use rules |
| Exclusivity | What conditions, review dates and exceptions apply? | Measurable commercial schedule |

## 2. Attach a SKU and claims schedule

The SKU schedule is the bridge between a distribution contract and the physical item. Give each sellable configuration a distinct identity. Record at least the supplier model, distributor SKU, hardware reference, approved firmware, enclosure, connector strategy, accessories, pack-out, manual revision, packaging artwork, barcode data and effective date.

Create a companion claims schedule. It should state the product category, wired-CarPlay prerequisite, approved feature descriptions, known limitations that need disclosure, and the path for an untested vehicle question. Avoid phrases such as “works with all cars” or blanket performance promises. A distributor may choose its marketing language, but both parties should know which statements have evidence and which must not be used.

The [sample approval checklist](/blog/oem-carplay-adapter-sample-approval-checklist/) is useful before the schedule is released. It helps buyers link the approved sample to configuration, functional scope, packaging and documented exceptions rather than approving an appearance alone.

## 3. Make forecasts, purchase orders and allocation rules explicit

A forecast is a planning signal; a purchase order is a commercial release. The agreement should not silently turn one into the other. Define the forecast horizon, update cadence, whether any portion is binding, the assumptions behind capacity discussions, and the point at which a PO is accepted.

Also define allocation when demand exceeds available supply. Questions include whether confirmed orders, paid deposits, safety stock, launch commitments or strategic accounts receive priority; who provides a revised schedule; and how the distributor is informed. This prevents sales teams from treating an indicative production conversation as confirmed availability.

The [supplier capacity and lead-time guide](/blog/wireless-carplay-adapter-supplier-capacity-lead-time/) explains why material, test, packaging and logistics dependencies need to be visible. For order-release details, use the separate [purchase-order checklist](/blog/wireless-carplay-adapter-purchase-order-checklist/) rather than repeating every PO field in the distributor agreement.

## 4. Separate price from the complete commercial cost

The unit price is only one commercial input. Specify the currency, price basis, quotation validity, payment milestones, taxes and duties assumptions, Incoterms version and named place, freight responsibility, insurance, bank charges, and treatment of samples, promotional units and replacement units. Do not assume a term is understood identically in every market.

For an importer, the agreement should also identify who owns product data needed for customs and local distribution: model identity, country-of-origin statements where applicable, carton counts, package dimensions, battery or radio declarations if relevant to the actual product, and supporting documents. The [landed-cost planning guide](/blog/wireless-carplay-adapter-landed-cost-planning/) can help teams model these inputs without inventing a margin or duty rate.

| Commercial topic | Agreement needs to say | Common risk if omitted |
|---|---|---|
| Price basis | Currency, included items, validity and revision rule | Quotes compared on different scopes |
| Handover | Incoterms version, named place and transfer points | Unclear freight, insurance or customs ownership |
| Payment | Trigger, method, documentation and overdue route | Production or shipment assumptions diverge |
| Service stock | Pricing and allocation for replacements or support units | Customer commitments cannot be fulfilled |

## 5. Control listings, packaging and localization

Distributors need enough flexibility to sell locally, but localized content cannot break the product identity. Set an approval route for product titles, compatibility statements, manuals, packaging changes, translations, marketplace copy, images and reseller asset packs. The goal is not to slow every listing edit; it is to make sure a substantive claim, instruction or model change reaches the right reviewer.

For private-label programs, identify who owns artwork source files, who approves print-ready artwork, how barcode ownership is handled, and what happens to obsolete cartons or old inventory. The [packaging approval checklist](/blog/private-label-carplay-adapter-packaging-approval-checklist/) and [manual localization guide](/blog/private-label-carplay-adapter-manual-localization/) provide the operating detail these clauses should reference.

## 6. Define delivery acceptance and document handover

Commercial handover and quality acceptance are related but not identical. State the shipment documents, carton labeling, batch or lot identity where used, visible-damage notification process, incoming-inspection window and how a discrepancy is recorded. Do not promise inspection outcomes that have not been agreed.

For a first delivery or a changed configuration, the distributor may need a retained reference sample and release documentation. The [incoming quality inspection guide](/blog/wireless-carplay-adapter-incoming-quality-inspection/) helps define a receiving gate that checks identity, pack-out, condition and planned functional screening. It does not replace supplier qualification or a controlled production process.

## 7. Build an after-sales escalation path before the first sale

Warranty language tells a customer what may be covered; an escalation path tells the parties how they will investigate and act. Agree the first-contact owner, diagnostic fields, target response expectations, replacement authorization, return routing, analysis responsibility and credit or settlement process. Keep these operational targets realistic and subject to the final contract.

An escalation record should identify the SKU, batch or order where available, hardware and firmware reference, vehicle and head-unit context, phone and operating-system state, cable or port path, symptom, steps to reproduce and supporting media. This is more useful than forwarding a vague report that an adapter “does not work.” The [warranty and RMA planning guide](/blog/wireless-carplay-adapter-warranty-rma-planning/) explains how to distinguish compatibility, setup and repeatable product-fault cases without making unsafe driving tests part of support.

## 8. Agree change control, data use and exit handling

Connected products evolve. The agreement should specify which changes require advance notice: hardware components, firmware, accessories, enclosure, packaging, manual, labeling, factory, inspection method or compliance evidence. Then define who assesses impact, how remaining inventory is identified, and when a revised listing or revalidation is needed. Never silently substitute a new configuration under an old approval record.

If support cases include customer contact details, vehicle information or device screenshots, include applicable privacy and data-processing responsibilities. Limit collection to the evidence needed for the case, define access and retention expectations, and use secure transfer methods appropriate to the parties’ obligations.

Finally, plan the end of the relationship: sell-off rights, trademark and asset removal, outstanding warranty handling, support access, spare or replacement stock, open orders, confidential material and product-change records. A clean exit plan protects buyers who already own the product.

## Distributor agreement pre-signing checklist

- [ ] Territory, permitted channels and any exclusivity conditions are written.
- [ ] Each sellable SKU maps to an approved hardware, firmware, accessory and packaging record.
- [ ] Approved claims state the wired-CarPlay prerequisite and avoid universal compatibility promises.
- [ ] Forecast, PO acceptance, allocation and schedule-update rules are separate and clear.
- [ ] Price, payment, Incoterms, freight, insurance, taxes and service-stock assumptions are documented.
- [ ] Local listing, packaging, manual and brand-asset approvals have named owners.
- [ ] Shipment documents, incoming inspection and discrepancy reporting are defined.
- [ ] Warranty, support, replacement and technical escalation owners are identified.
- [ ] Product, firmware, packaging and documentation changes trigger controlled notification.
- [ ] Privacy, confidentiality, sell-off and post-termination service responsibilities are reviewed by appropriate advisers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a distributor agreement the same as a purchase order?

No. The distributor agreement sets the continuing commercial and operating rules. A purchase order releases a defined quantity, configuration, price and delivery scope under those rules or another agreed framework.

### Should a distributor receive exclusive rights automatically?

Not automatically. Exclusivity is a negotiated commercial choice. If used, it should define territory, channels, conditions, review dates, exceptions and what happens if conditions are not met.

### How should a distributor describe compatibility?

State the actual prerequisite: a compatible factory wired-CarPlay host through the intended USB data path. Describe the validated scope and provide a route to assess uncertain vehicles. Do not promise compatibility with every vehicle, phone or head unit.

### Who is responsible when firmware changes?

The agreement should allocate notice, impact review, validation, documentation, inventory identification and support responsibilities. The exact allocation depends on the program, but a firmware change should not be invisible to channel content or support teams.

### What should happen to warranty cases after an agreement ends?

Plan it before signing. Define customer-facing ownership, support access, remaining replacement stock, technical records, return routing and any agreed sell-off or service period, subject to applicable law and contract review.
