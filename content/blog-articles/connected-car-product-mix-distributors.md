**Direct answer:** a connected-car distributor should build its product mix around distinct buyer problems rather than collecting similar-looking devices. Wireless CarPlay adapters serve vehicles that already have compatible factory wired CarPlay. CarPlay AI boxes target compatible factory displays where buyers want an Android-based application platform. Portable smart screens address vehicles or use cases that need an independent display. Define one role, buyer profile, compatibility boundary, price position and support workflow for every SKU; then start with a controlled assortment that produces reliable sales and return evidence before expanding.

The commercial goal is not to display the largest possible catalog. It is to give sales teams, resellers and end buyers a clear route from vehicle condition to the right product category. A disciplined mix reduces duplicate inventory, unclear claims and avoidable support tickets while preserving enough choice for meaningful channel coverage.

## Start with buyer problems, not supplier model lists

Supplier catalogs often organize products by chipset, enclosure or release sequence. Distributors need a market-facing structure. Begin with the job the buyer is trying to complete:

- remove the phone cable from an existing wired CarPlay experience;
- add applications or local media capability to a compatible factory display;
- add a separate CarPlay-capable display to a vehicle that lacks a suitable factory platform;
- support a motorcycle, fleet, camper or other installation with different mounting and power needs;
- offer a good-better-best range without creating indistinguishable SKUs.

This problem-first view prevents a common assortment mistake: placing products in the same category because they connect by USB even though their prerequisites, operating systems, installation and support questions differ.

## Define the three main category roles

| Category | Primary buyer problem | Typical prerequisite | Important validation questions | Main commercial risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wireless CarPlay adapter | Convert an existing wired experience to wireless | Functioning compatible factory wired CarPlay | Vehicle host, USB data port, phone, startup, controls and recovery | Selling to vehicles without the required host |
| CarPlay AI box | Add an Android-based application platform through a compatible factory interface | Product-specific compatible factory display and interface | Platform, memory, apps, region, heat, boot, controls and update ownership | Treating every AI box as interchangeable |
| Portable smart screen | Add an independent display and connected interface | Suitable mounting, power and audio path | Screen size, mount, visibility, power, audio, camera options and installation | Underestimating installation and accessory complexity |

A wireless adapter normally does not create CarPlay in a vehicle that lacks the necessary wired host. The guide on [vehicles without factory wired CarPlay](/blog/wireless-carplay-without-factory-wired-carplay/) explains why portable screens or head-unit replacement may be more suitable in those cases.

AI boxes and basic wireless adapters also should not share one sales promise. Review the architectural differences in [CarPlay AI box vs wireless CarPlay adapter](/blog/carplay-ai-box-vs-wireless-carplay-adapter/) before defining category names, training and support scripts.

## Give every SKU a specific portfolio job

Each SKU should earn its place by covering a distinct combination of buyer, channel and use case. A useful SKU-role statement can be written in one sentence:

> This model is the [entry/core/premium/specialist] option for [buyer or channel] that needs [specific outcome] under [compatibility boundary].

Examples of legitimate differentiation include connector configuration, platform capability, screen size, installation format, regional requirement, accessory package, retail presentation or support tier. A different enclosure color alone may not justify a separate stocked SKU unless the channel can sell and forecast it independently.

For every proposed model, record:

- target customer and sales channel;
- product category and non-negotiable prerequisite;
- entry, core, premium or specialist role;
- price band and expected margin structure;
- included cable, mount, power and other accessories;
- packaging language and required claims;
- validation scope and excluded applications;
- service replacement and firmware responsibility;
- minimum order, reorder trigger and phase-out rule.

If two models have the same role, promise, buyer and price band, the portfolio may be carrying duplication rather than choice.

## Build a category ladder instead of a flat catalog

A category ladder helps buyers understand why one option costs more. The levels must be based on verified differences, not vague labels.

### Entry

The entry SKU should solve the core problem with limited complexity. Keep the compatibility message explicit, the accessory pack controlled and the setup path easy to support. Entry does not mean untested; it means the feature scope is intentionally focused.

### Core

The core SKU is the model the channel can explain and replenish most consistently. It should cover the representative use cases, have a stable approved configuration and generate enough volume to justify training, content and service stock.

### Premium

Premium positioning needs an observable reason: a higher platform specification, additional interface capability, stronger accessory set, different screen or installation format, or a service package that the target channel values. Avoid charging a premium for specifications that the buyer cannot use or verify.

### Specialist

Specialist products serve a narrower application such as a motorcycle format, fleet requirement, unusual mounting condition or regional platform. They may be commercially valuable without being high-volume, but inventory and sales ownership should be explicit.

## Score demand quality before assigning inventory

Forecasts should distinguish evidence from optimism. A product with many page views but repeated compatibility confusion may require better qualification before more stock. A specialist item with fewer orders may still be valuable if its buyer fit, margin and return behavior are strong.

Use a simple evidence table by SKU and channel:

| Evidence | What to record | Decision supported |
|---|---|---|
| Qualified inquiries | Vehicle condition, requested outcome and buyer type | Whether the category solves a real problem |
| Sample results | Exact configuration, test scope and limitations | Whether the product is ready for a channel pilot |
| Conversion | Quoted, ordered and declined opportunities with reason | Whether positioning and price are understandable |
| Returns and tickets | Failure category, compatibility error, setup issue or defect evidence | Whether to revise training, claims or product |
| Reorder pattern | Sell-through interval and channel concentration | Whether to increase, hold or reduce inventory |
| Margin after support | Gross margin adjusted for service and replacement burden | Whether the SKU is commercially sustainable |

Do not invent a universal sell-through threshold. Product category, region, sales channel and support model change the result. Agree on a review cadence and compare each SKU with its planned role.

## Plan assortment breadth and inventory depth separately

Breadth is the number of distinct needs the catalog can address. Depth is how much inventory the distributor holds for each approved SKU. They should not be expanded at the same time without evidence.

A practical launch sequence is:

1. Select a small number of clearly separated category roles.
2. Validate samples and compatibility boundaries.
3. Prepare sales qualification and support material.
4. Allocate a controlled first order by channel.
5. Track inquiries, conversion, returns and reorders.
6. Add depth to proven core models.
7. Add breadth only where a documented demand gap remains.

## Include support load in the product-mix decision

Two products with similar invoice margin can create very different operating costs. Product pages, manuals, compatibility qualification, firmware ownership, installation guidance, ticket diagnosis and replacements all consume channel resources.

Before approving an SKU, ask:

- Can sales explain the prerequisite in one sentence?
- Can support identify the model, hardware and firmware?
- Is there a repeatable direct-wired or installation baseline?
- Are the most common wrong-fit scenarios documented?
- Can the supplier reproduce escalated issues from evidence?
- Are update, warranty and replacement responsibilities defined?
- Can old and new revisions be distinguished in inventory?

This is especially important when mixing adapters, AI boxes and smart screens. Each category needs its own qualification questions. One generic “works with CarPlay” support script is not enough.

## Design the first channel launch

Different channels need different assortment logic.

**Online retail** benefits from explicit prerequisites, comparison tables, vehicle-condition questions and strong self-service setup content. Too many nearly identical SKUs can reduce confidence and increase wrong purchases.

**Installers and automotive shops** may support more specialist products because staff can inspect the vehicle, choose mounting and power paths, and demonstrate operation. Training and accessory availability matter more than a broad public catalog.

**Distributors serving resellers** need controlled product data, stable SKU mapping, regional packaging, replenishment visibility and escalation routes. A model that cannot be described consistently across reseller feeds creates downstream errors.

**Private-label programs** should connect the portfolio decision to branding, packaging, validation, change control and after-sales ownership. TrolinkTek’s [OEM/ODM workflow](/oem-odm/) outlines the stages from requirement definition through production and support.

## Prepare a product-mix RFQ

Instead of requesting a price list for every model, send a role-based RFQ. For each required portfolio position, provide:

- target market and channel;
- customer problem and intended use;
- required product category;
- compatibility prerequisite and excluded cases;
- target price position without hiding required scope;
- expected order or forecast band;
- branding, packaging and language requirements;
- sample and representative validation plan;
- required commercial and technical evidence;
- desired launch date and approval owners.

Ask the supplier to recommend a configuration for each role and identify overlap. The response should explain assumptions, not merely attach unrelated product sheets. ## Distributor product-mix checklist

- Define buyer problems before choosing models.
- Separate adapters, AI boxes and smart screens by architecture.
- Assign one clear portfolio role to every SKU.
- Document compatibility prerequisites and exclusions.
- Create entry, core, premium and specialist logic only where evidence supports it.
- Separate assortment breadth from inventory depth.
- Include support and replacement load in margin review.
- Launch a controlled assortment before expanding.
- Track qualified inquiries, conversion, returns and reorders.
- Use role-based RFQs and controlled sample approval.
- Define reorder, revision and phase-out rules.
- Link the final assortment to channel training and product content.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many connected-car products should a distributor launch?

There is no universal number. Start with the smallest assortment that covers distinct, validated buyer problems without duplicate roles. Expand after conversion, return, support and reorder evidence shows a real gap.

### Should a distributor sell adapters, AI boxes and smart screens together?

They can form a useful portfolio because they address different vehicle conditions and buyer outcomes. The categories must be clearly separated by prerequisite, capability, installation and support process.

### What is the difference between assortment breadth and inventory depth?

Breadth is how many distinct needs or SKU roles the catalog covers. Depth is the stock held for each SKU. A distributor can test broader demand with controlled quantities while placing deeper inventory behind proven core products.

### How can a distributor reduce product overlap?

Write a role statement for every SKU and compare buyer, use case, compatibility boundary, price position, accessories and support scope. If two models cannot be differentiated with verified buyer value, consolidate or test one at a time.

### Which product should be the core SKU?

The core SKU should match the most important qualified demand, have a stable approved configuration, be explainable by the channel and show acceptable conversion, support and reorder behavior. It is not automatically the cheapest or most feature-rich model.

### What should be sent to a supplier before requesting recommendations?

Send target markets, channels, customer problems, vehicle prerequisites, category roles, price positions, forecast bands, customization needs, validation expectations and support requirements. This lets recommendations address a commercial plan rather than a generic catalog.
